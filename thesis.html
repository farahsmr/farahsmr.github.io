Bachelor's Thesis

Empirical Evaluation of Query Optimization Techniques Using Controlled Execution Plans

Abstract

Query optimizers work in an interesting manner, almost like a chain of connected choices. Several decisions are brought together until one executable plan is selected as the best according to the information available to the optimizer. Understanding these decisions is essential, which is why work on more accurate estimators, better search strategies or better operator rules never really stops. Much of this work, however, focuses on individual decisions separately. In this thesis, we consider both views and examine the decisions individually and as part of the optimization process. To realize this experimentally, we adopt recently developed research frameworks that give us a certain level of control over selected optimizer decisions, while leaving the remaining optimization process to PostgreSQL. We consider three benchmarks, and the results show differences between workloads and cases where some decisions are more important than others, where local improvements do not translate into better final plans, and where promising changes or distorted information interact with other decisions and produce interesting results. Rather than proposing a new optimization algorithm, this thesis is structured around this interplay, highlighting that understanding it can sometimes be just as important as improving a single decision, if not more. Our experiments are limited to PostgreSQL, but the way we ask the questions is more general: as optimizer techniques and systems continue to change, understanding how individual optimizer decisions affect performance, and how these effects change once the decisions interact, can be useful beyond one particular system or database version.
